# Visual Hierarchy

## Principle

Visual hierarchy is the organization of elements so their relative importance can be understood through perception before every detail is read.

## Why it matters

A page is not experienced as a flat list of components. People notice, group, scan, and then inspect. If everything competes equally, the user must do unnecessary work to discover what matters.

Hierarchy therefore serves both communication and usability.

## Recognition

Look for:

- multiple elements competing for first attention
- primary and secondary information that appear equally important
- weak distinction between sections
- inconsistent emphasis
- important actions visually buried
- decorative elements louder than content
- hierarchy changing unpredictably between similar screens

Common hierarchy levers include:

- size
- weight
- contrast
- position
- whitespace
- repetition
- alignment
- scale
- imagery
- motion

These are tools, not rules. Increasing size is not automatically the right answer.

## Question for a non-designer

Instead of:

> What should have the highest visual hierarchy?

Ask:

> If someone looks at this page for two seconds, what do you most want them to notice first?

Then:

> What should they understand second?

## Decision

For every major page or section, establish:

1. primary message/action
2. supporting information
3. navigation/context
4. optional detail
5. decoration

The visual treatment should reinforce that order.

## Action

Use a small number of strong differences rather than many competing ones.

Example:

```text
Primary
  large / strong / high contrast

Secondary
  moderate / supporting contrast

Tertiary
  quiet / compact
```

A good hierarchy often feels obvious without feeling aggressive.

## Gestalt connection

Hierarchy is strengthened by grouping and perceptual organization. Proximity, similarity, figure/ground, alignment, and continuity can communicate relationships before users read labels.

## Critique

Ask:

- Where does my eye land first?
- Is that where the content strategy says it should land?
- What competes with it?
- Can I identify the primary action without reading everything?
- Are similar things visually treated as similar?
- Does every section have an intentional entry point?

## Agent rule

When a design feels "busy", do not immediately reduce the number of elements. First determine whether the real problem is weak hierarchy, poor grouping, insufficient contrast, or excessive decoration.
